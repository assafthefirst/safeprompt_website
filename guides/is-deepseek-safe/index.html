Is DeepSeek safe to use?

Updated October 29, 2026, 7-minute read

Short answer: DeepSeek is a capable AI model, but its chat app and website come with privacy trade-offs worth understanding before you paste anything personal into them. What you type is stored on servers in China, the privacy policy sets no fixed time limit for keeping it, and several governments have restricted the app on official devices. If you use it, keep real names, numbers and work data out of your prompts.

Where your data goes

DeepSeek's privacy policy says the information it collects is stored on servers in the People's Republic of China, and its terms are governed by Chinese law. For many people that's the deciding factor, because it means your conversations fall under a different legal system from the one you live in, with different rules on government access.

What DeepSeek collects

According to its privacy policy, as reported by Tom's Guide, DeepSeek collects:

  • Everything you send: prompts, text and audio input, uploaded files, feedback and chat history
  • Device and technical data: IP address, device model, cookies, and device and user IDs that can link your activity across devices
  • Keystroke patterns or rhythms, listed as technical information it collects
  • Account and payment details, if you sign in or pay

On retention, the policy says DeepSeek keeps data "as long as necessary" and doesn't set a fixed time limit.

The 2025 database exposure

In January 2025, security researchers at Wiz found a DeepSeek database that was publicly accessible on the internet with no password. It contained over a million lines of log data, including chat history, API keys and backend details. DeepSeek secured it promptly after being notified, but it showed how quickly conversation data can end up somewhere it was never meant to be.

Why governments restricted it

Within weeks of DeepSeek's launch, several governments acted:

  • Italy's data protection authority blocked the app in January 2025 over how it handles personal data.
  • South Korea suspended new downloads for about two months in 2025, after its privacy regulator found DeepSeek had broken data protection rules in its app rollout.
  • Taiwan, Australia and several US agencies and states barred it on government devices.
  • Ireland and Belgium opened privacy investigations.

Most of these actions target official devices and data, not personal use. But they show how privacy regulators assess the risk.

Is DeepSeek safe for personal use?

It depends on what you share. Asking DeepSeek to explain a concept, write code that contains no secrets, or brainstorm ideas carries little risk. Pasting a client email, a contract, an ID number or your health details is a different matter, because that information leaves your control the moment you press Send, and you can't easily get it back.

Avoid sharing with DeepSeek:

  • Names together with contact details, yours or anyone else's
  • ID, passport, card and bank numbers
  • Health, legal or financial details about real people
  • Work material: client data, contracts, source code with keys, internal plans

If your employer has an AI policy, check it first. Many organizations have banned DeepSeek on work devices.

How to use DeepSeek more safely

  1. Use placeholders. Replace real details with labels like [NAME] and [EMAIL] before you send. The answer is just as useful (step-by-step method).
  2. Don't upload original files. A PDF or screenshot sends everything in it, including details you didn't notice.
  3. Use the web version rather than the app if you want to limit device data collection, and don't grant permissions you don't need.
  4. Consider running the model locally. DeepSeek's models are openly available, so tech-savvy users can run them on their own computer with tools such as Ollama. In that case, what you type doesn't go to DeepSeek's servers at all.

Key takeaways

  • DeepSeek stores what you type on servers in China, with no fixed deletion date.
  • Its policy lists keystroke patterns and device identifiers among the data it collects.
  • A 2025 exposure leaked chat history, and several governments restricted the app.
  • For anything personal or work-related, use placeholders, or run the model locally.
